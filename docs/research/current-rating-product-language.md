# Current language for rating founder fit

Research date: 2026-08-04

## Ranked conclusion

The strongest direction is **one slangy risk signal, four plain founder
capabilities, and one memorable summary question**:

1. **LARP** — How much of the founder persona was backed by work or results the
   reviewer personally saw?
2. **Technical depth** — Could they understand and make strong technical calls,
   not merely repeat the vocabulary?
3. **Product taste** — Could they tell what was worth building and what “good”
   looked like?
4. **Velocity** — Did the team learn, decide, and make real progress quickly?
5. **Can they sell?** — Could they create demand: customers first, and when
   relevant talent, partners, or capital?
6. **Would you run it back?** — Yes / Maybe / No.

This is a vocabulary recommendation, not yet a validated assessment model. The
labels should be tested with actual founders before they become product canon.
Each capability needs **Not enough exposure**; a reviewer should not score a
technical or commercial dimension they did not directly observe.

The key style choice is not to force every item into a branded `___ Score`.
`LARP`, `Technical depth`, `Product taste`, `Velocity`, `Can they sell?`, and
`Run it back?` sound like questions founders might actually ask one another.
Names such as “Raccoon Score,” “Founder Gravity,” and “Cockroach Score” sound
written for a startup-themed party game.

## What adjacent products actually do

### One decisive counterfactual beats a synthetic overall score

Rate My Professors separates **Would Take Again** from Overall Quality and
Difficulty. Its public profiles lead with the percentage who would take the
professor again and preserve the reviewer’s Yes/No answer on each review. That
is the closest proven interaction pattern to **Would you run it back?**
([rating scale](https://help.ratemyprofessors.com/article/31-rating-scale),
[current profile example](https://www.ratemyprofessors.com/professor/365099)).

Startup reference checks use almost the same counterfactual. First Round’s
current reference guide recommends “Would you rehire this person for almost any
role?” and the blunter “Would you hire this person without question again?” It
also recommends asking for the conditions under which the answer changes
([First Round reference guide](https://review.firstround.com/25-questions-for-reference-calls/)).

**Borrow:** Make **Run it back?** the headline percentage and keep the per-review
answer attached to relationship context. Use **Yes / Maybe / No**, with an
optional short “What would need to be different?” follow-up for Maybe or No.

### Successful rating products use boring categories and vivid tags

Rate My Professors uses literal quantitative labels—Overall Quality and Level
of Difficulty—then lets controlled tags provide texture: “Tough grader,”
“Amazing lectures,” “Gives good feedback,” and “Get ready to read.” Glassdoor
similarly uses literal factor names such as Career Opportunities, Culture &
Values, Senior Management, and Work/Life Balance
([RMP profile example](https://www.ratemyprofessors.com/professor/1216762),
[Glassdoor factor list](https://www.glassdoor.com/about/press-release/glassdoor-launches-advanced-filters-to-find-companies-highly-rated-for-work-life-balance-diversity-amp-inclusion-and-more/)).
Blind’s current company pages follow the same plain-label pattern: Career
Growth, Work Life Balance, Compensation / Benefits, Company Culture, and
Management
([Blind profile example](https://www.teamblind.com/company/ByteDance/)).

**Borrow:** Keep the dimensions readable without explanatory copy. Put the
rascal energy into **LARP**, **Run it back?**, controlled tags, empty states,
and answer anchors—not six invented mascots.

### Founder language is functional: build, product, sell, move

YC Co-Founder Matching asks whether someone is technical and which functions
they can own: Product, Design, Engineering, Sales and Marketing, and Operations.
It defines a technical founder concretely as someone who can build the product
without outside assistance. YC also gives more weight to a specific impressive
thing someone built or achieved than to generic claims about dedication
([matching data](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/what-do-people-want-in-a-co-founder),
[application guidance](https://www.ycombinator.com/howtoapply.html)).

Recent founder discussion is similarly blunt. A 2025 Hacker News debate reduces
the complementary founder jobs to being able to **build**, **sell**, **market**,
and **refine**; the disagreement is about which founder does them, not the
vocabulary
([Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=43814497)).

**Borrow:** Prefer **Technical depth** or the prompt **Can they build?** over
“Technical Chops” or “Wizard.” Prefer **Can they sell?** over “Founder Gravity.”
The former names an observable job; the latter makes a personality myth out of
several unrelated outcomes.

### “Taste,” “velocity,” and “LARP” are live vocabulary—but need anchors

`Product taste` and `velocity` appear in current founder and technical
discussion without needing a joke wrapped around them. A recent investor
description uses “intense urgency” and “intellectual velocity” for learning and
iteration; a current Hacker News discussion explicitly argues that technical
**taste** is more differentiating in the vibe-coding era because speed without
judgment produces fragile systems
([Northside founder criteria](https://www.northside.ventures/perspectives/the-northside-approach-our-vision-for-backing-exceptional-canadian-founders-before-day-one),
[Hacker News taste discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=45739499)).

`LARP` is also genuinely present in current startup-community language, usually
to distinguish the trappings of a startup from actual work, product, customers,
or commitment. In a heavily discussed 2025 r/startups thread, commenters call a
pre-seed organization with many executive titles, unpaid contributors, and no
clear substance “LARPing a startup”
([r/startups thread](https://www.reddit.com/r/startups/comments/1ltkntt/)).

**Borrow with constraints:**

- **LARP** should measure the gap between claims and observed substance, not
  whether the reviewer finds someone annoying.
- **Product taste** should ask about decisions the reviewer saw: what to build,
  what to cut, and whether the resulting product was good.
- **Velocity** must mean progress or learning per unit time, not visible busyness
  or unhealthy working hours.

## Candidate review copy

This copy is deliberately direct rather than “brand voice” applied to every
line.

| Display label | Review question | Suggested five-position anchors |
|---|---|---|
| **LARP** | How much of the founder persona was backed by work or results you personally saw? | All receipts · Mostly real · Mixed · Mostly LARP · Full LARP |
| **Technical depth** | How strong were their technical understanding and technical decisions? | Out of depth · Surface level · Solid · Deep · Exceptional |
| **Product taste** | How good were they at knowing what to build, what to cut, and what “good” looked like? | No signal · Missed often · Mixed · Usually right · Sharp |
| **Velocity** | How quickly did they turn decisions into learning or real progress? | Stalled · Slow · Normal · Fast · Relentless |
| **Can they sell?** | Could they get customers—and when relevant talent, partners, or investors—to commit? | No signal · Struggled · Sometimes · Usually · Absolutely |
| **Run it back?** | Knowing what you know now, would you found or work closely with them again? | Yes · Maybe · No |

The exact anchors need copy testing. In particular, **Relentless** can reward
burnout and **All receipts** can become stale slang. The underlying behavioral
questions matter more than those endpoint words.

### Optional controlled tags

Rate My Professors demonstrates that tags are better than multiplying rating
axes. A small founder-native set could add texture without pretending to be a
scientific score:

- **Can build**
- **Can sell**
- **Good taste**
- **Moves fast**
- **Deep in the weeds**
- **Great in a crisis**
- **All deck**
- **Title collector**

Tags should only summarize conduct described in the review and should not feed
a universal composite score.

## What to avoid

1. **Mascot metrics:** Raccoon, Cockroach, Gravity, Wizard. These are invented
   brand metaphors, not current judgment language.
2. **VC cliché as product taxonomy:** high agency, grit, resilience, founder
   mode, visionary, hustle. They are common, but too broad to tell a reader what
   happened. “High agency” and “grit” are especially easy to use as flattering
   vibes with no falsifiable behavior.
3. **Role prejudice disguised as fitness:** A nontechnical founder is not
   automatically low quality. Technical depth is useful only when the reviewer
   observed it and when technical contribution was relevant to the relationship.
4. **One Founder Score:** YC’s own co-founder material separates functional
   ownership, commitment, working preferences, goals, and conflict rather than
   pretending they collapse into one trait. It also recommends a trial project
   and reference calls because compatibility requires actual exposure
   ([YC co-founder questions](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/10-questions-to-discuss-with-a-potential-co-founder)).
5. **Unanchored numbers:** First Round’s guide notes that the useful information
   comes from the follow-up—why the person was not rated higher or lower—and
   from a concrete project that depended on them. The review should therefore
   require a first-hand example behind unusually high or low ratings
   ([reference guide](https://review.firstround.com/25-questions-for-reference-calls/)).
6. **Personality or moral diagnosis:** Rate My Professors tells reviewers to
   focus on their own experience, avoid hearsay, and provide substantive
   comments. That constraint matters even more for founder reviews
   ([RMP guidelines](https://www.ratemyprofessors.com/guidelines)).

## Recommended product pattern

Use five dimensions at most on the initial review. Show **Run it back?** as the
headline outcome, the five dimensions as a relationship-specific shape, and
two or three controlled tags as the memorable texture. Require **Not enough
exposure** everywhere except Run it back. Ask for a concrete incident after any
extreme answer.

If the flow needs to be even lighter, test this four-item cut first:

1. **LARP**
2. **Technical depth**
3. **Velocity**
4. **Run it back?**

Then collect **Product taste** and **Can they sell?** only when the stated
relationship gave the reviewer direct exposure to those areas.
